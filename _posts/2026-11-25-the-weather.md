I'm getting caught up on lost time with a bit of space in my schedule and empty-nesting for the moment. We learned about the weather and, with it, were further acclimated with the idea that Korean conjugates verbs and what we English speakers see as adjectives much the same way.

No matter, here are the basic weather descriptions we used. Pair with the seasons 봄 (spring), 여름 (summer), 가을 (autumn), and 겨울 (winter).


| **English** | **Korean** | **Literal English** |
|:-----------:|:----------:|:--------------------|
| It's clear | 맑아요 | It's clear. |
| It's cloudy | 흐려요  | It's cloudy. | 
| It's windy | 바람이 부어요 | The wind is blowing. |
| It's raining | 비가 와요 | The rain is coming. |
| It's snowing | 눈이 와요 | The snow is coming. |
| It's warm | 따뜻애요 | It has the quality of warmth.[^hada] |
| It's hot | 더워요 | It is hot. |
| It's cold | 추워요 | It is cold. |
| It's cool | 시원해요 | It has the quality of being cool/refreshing.[^siwon] |
| It's chilly | 쌀쌀해요 | It has the quality of being uncomfortably cold.[^mimetic] |



[^mimetic]: I was aware these were in the wild, but I'm very sure this is the first one we went over in class. Korean makes use of what are called ["mimetic words" (의태어)](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%EC%9D%98%ED%83%9C%EC%96%B4). In this case, I'm fairly sure 쌀쌀 is a sound-symbolic expression that serves as a visual representation of what it describes: to be shaking/shivering a bit because it's a bit cold and you should've brought a jacket.

[^siwon]: This verb has multiple meanings, for which "cool" is rather all-encompassing from an English speaker's perspective. You could use it describe a lot of things. Even "속이 시원해요" would mean something like "I feel relieved."


[^hada]: This is admittedly my limited understanding of Korean, but I keep wanting to read 하다 as analogous to the Spanish *hacer*. Both are routinely used as verbs in the context of doing/making. However, 하다 also creates a descriptive predicate. So, perhaps, the literal way of thinking about what this is doing is to think (perhaps wrongly?) that the root here is 따뜻 and is a noun communicating "warmth", but isn't a complete predicate in modern/standard Korean. 하다 completes this incomplete concept by describing a state or quality that exists. Supposedly, this convention recurs a fair bit in Korean words with Chinese origins (e.g. to study, to exercise), but this is a bit of an exception.
